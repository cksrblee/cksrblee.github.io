## 1. The Failure of the Metaverse and Extended Reality

Why did the metaverse fail? The metaverse is an umbrella term for various forms and content that realize virtual reality, referring to 3D spatial platforms where the physical and virtual worlds are combined. Encompassing Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and Extended Reality (hereafter “XR”), which builds on the concept of extending reality into the virtual realm, the metaverse rose to prominence as a new frontier when a contactless society suddenly arrived during the pandemic.

However, the metaverse enjoyed only a brief COVID-19 boom. Because of its vague definition, it lacked core content capable of revolutionarily transforming people’s everyday lives, and it drew criticism for being indistinguishable from well-known games like *Minecraft*—leading many to declare it a failure. For example, Meta—which staked its future on the metaverse to the point of changing its name from Facebook—recorded $4.2 billion in losses in Reality Labs, its metaverse research and business division, with cumulative losses since 2020 exceeding $60 billion, prompting restructuring of its metaverse unit alongside a strategic pivot toward AI.

Perhaps for that reason, in 2024 Apple advocated for “Spatial Computing” instead of the familiar term “metaverse,” narrowing the concept closer to XR while emphasizing practical value. Spatial Computing is a technology that connects the physical world and the digital world to deliver an immersive and natural computing experience, a concept closely aligned with XR. Although Apple’s initial attempt could be seen as unsuccessful—with its accompanying XR headset, Vision Pro, reportedly struggling in sales and prompting preparations for a more affordable consumer version—the underlying technological vision at the heart of XR, connecting and converging the real and virtual worlds to create tangible innovation, still looks poised to become a massive wave in the years ahead.

## 2. The Problems with the Metaverse

Since 2021, while researching XR-based, operator-friendly interfaces for mobility systems such as unmanned aerial vehicles and autonomous vehicles, I developed a deep interest in XR and began asking myself: *what would a metaverse destined to succeed look like?* Looking back on my university coursework, the metaverse training program offered by the Gyeonggi Content Agency, and various hands-on activities studying related technologies, non-XR metaverses suffered from two major problems.

First, it was hard to feel any real difference between the metaverse and 3D games. In fact, many services labeled as “metaverse” faced heavy criticism because limitations in production budgets and computing power left them with worse graphics than existing 3D games. Furthermore, the fact that most mobile-based metaverse platforms relied on touch-based controllers and UIs virtually identical to 3D games likely made the quality gap compared to popular mobile titles even more apparent.

Second, the question of what practical innovation the metaverse could offer beyond entertainment remained unanswered. Although metaverse-style social networks such as ZEPETO and Bondee achieved significant popularity in 2022, it was disappointing that their appeal stopped at entertainment. Meanwhile, some platforms attempted to deliver tangible benefits by pairing the metaverse with cryptocurrency to build creator-driven economies under the “Play-to-Earn (P2E)” model, but this left them open to criticism as speculative, gambling-like games. As these and other efforts fell short, the lack of a true “killer application”—something capable of attracting users and delivering genuine value—persisted.

In 2023, I built *BID:AM (Bid Art Metaverse)*, a mobile-based metaverse SNS where users could create personal auction houses to auction and share NFTs. After winning the Korea Metaverse Industry Association President’s Award at the Metaverse Developer Contest—a competition with a 200 million KRW total prize pool hosted by the Korea Radio Promotion Association (RAPA)—I attempted to turn it into a startup. While operating the service, I tried to overcome the very limitations described above, but after a few months, I ultimately had to give up the service.

Yet it was precisely because of this failure of the metaverse that I became an ardent supporter of XR. Unlike the broad notion of the metaverse, XR is fundamentally an interface technology centered on its user—the human—that can reflect both physical space and user context. My conviction grew stronger that if we advance XR around real human needs, such as cognitive limits and physical constraints, it can drive practical innovation for humanity on par with the innovation brought by the smartphone. In fact, this has been my goal ever since I took immersive media courses at university and fell deeply in love with *Virtual Reality* and *HCI*, drawn by how they are not only interdisciplinary—combining computer science with psychology, cognitive science, and other fields—but also centered on people.

## 3. XR’s Greatest Strength: “Personalization”

Through a headset, an XR system reflects the user’s field of view and movements in real time while connecting physical space and digital information. Beyond merely providing visual information, it enables tailored interactions that reflect the user’s context and situation—giving XR far greater potential as a “personalized interface” than the conventional metaverse.

Just before my internship, while writing my book *Tech in CES 2023 for Super Rookie: Web3.0 & Metaverse*, I interviewed VR expert Prof. Hyeongseok Kim, who pointed to “personalization” as a major advantage the virtual world holds over the physical world. As he explained, *“Unlike reality, virtual reality can change to fit a user’s needs and characteristics, making it possible to provide a customized user experience.”* This implies that because the virtual spaces and objects XR overlays onto reality can reflect a user’s physical viewpoint, context, and surrounding environment, the user’s current physical space itself can be personalized. For example, even within the exact same room, the function of the space can be transformed according to context—highlighting cooking utensils when the user is cooking, or guiding lighting when the user is reading.

For this level of personalization to be realized, however, an XR system must be able to interpret the meaning and context of the surrounding space and interact proactively with the user. Simply tracking a user’s behavior is not enough. True personalization happens only when the system can think about *what this space is for*, *what the user is doing right now*, and *what they need to do*. In other words, AI that understands space, converses, and responds must be integrated into XR.

In early 2023, while interning at a startup, I planned a project that deployed robots inside water and sewage pipelines—part of South Korea’s national SOC infrastructure—to collect exploration data and build AI training datasets. Taking responsibility for writing the research proposal and leading a ~1 billion KRW research initiative even as an intern was a great experience in itself, but even more valuable was how it helped me find the direction of AI research I wanted to pursue. Inside the pipelines, rapid water currents made robot control difficult, and images captured by shaking cameras with poor visibility struggled to capture the context or meaning of the site. AI for XR faces a parallel challenge: because it perceives a 3D world through an XR headset, AI in XR must be able to autonomously interpret the surrounding physical environment, respond appropriately, and communicate with the user in real time.

This kind of AI is called **Embodied AI**. Embodied AI refers to next-generation AI that enables an agent to perceive and act like a human within a physical environment. Similar to the concept of “Physical AI” gaining prominence in 2025, Embodied AI—put differently—is artificial intelligence capable of perceiving real-world scenes, identifying actionable points within them, thinking and reasoning like a human, and responding appropriately to context.

Following that internship, thanks to a recommendation from a director who saw my potential, I got the opportunity to work as an AI Engineer at a generative AI service company. Working as an LLM engineer, I watched LLMs continuously evolve into AI agents equipped with reasoning capabilities and various tools. It occurred to me that if an agent could understand 3D space—along with its functional elements and meaning—we could personalize space itself, which led me to decide to attend graduate school. In short, I formed a new goal: to research a “succeeding XR” by building Embodied AI-based systems that interpret 3D environments and interact with users, thereby maximizing XR’s potential for personalization.

## 4. Embodied AI-Based Extended Reality Research

With this goal in mind, during my M.S. in Computer Science (Artificial Intelligence) at USC, I aim to broaden my academic depth under the guidance of Prof. Ulrich Neumann, who leads the CGIT Lab, focusing on the core technologies for Embodied AI—**3D Scene Understanding**, **Vision-Language-Action Models (VLA)**, and **XR Adaptive AI**—and to grow as a researcher by publishing related research results through the Thesis Option.

First, in **3D Scene Understanding**, I am paying close attention to the shift emphasized at major conferences such as CVPR: moving beyond the limitations of conventional scene understanding, which stopped at object recognition or localization, toward spatial understanding grounded in physical interaction. Building on **Intermediate Representations** that structure spaces into AI-interpretable forms and **Goal-conditioned Scene Abstraction**, my goal is to design XR-based Embodied AI agents capable of real-time interaction.

Through **VLA** research connected to this, I want to study technologies that enable AI to understand scenes within the user’s field of view and respond to verbal queries or react with actions. In particular, given that VLAs are recently advancing on top of LLMs, I am also interested in designing fast-reasoning models, model compression for faster response times, and designing protocols that connect spatial perception directly to action.

Finally, in what I call **XR Adaptive AI**, I want to research adaptive AI that infers user state using first-person viewpoint data, flexibly adjusts response strategies depending on the situation, and incorporates long-term preference learning architectures. My goal is to design systems that identify objects the user is focusing on or detect states of confusion based on gaze—adjusting the amount and form of feedback—and to implement architectures that learn an individual’s response patterns through repeated interactions, gradually evolving into a personalized AI.

Among the many schools I could choose for studying abroad, the biggest reason I chose USC is that it is not an unfamiliar place to me. In the summer of 2022, I first encountered AI and data science at USC through a university summer program, and that experience planted the dream of going back to the United States to study again. Because I wished so earnestly to return here, I believe that continuing my studies at USC will allow me to pursue my work with even deeper immersion and passion.

Moreover, USC is located in Los Angeles, where not only technology but also the entertainment industry thrives, and offers joint courses with the School of Cinematic Arts—one of the world’s top three film schools—providing opportunities to do team projects with students from other majors in VR/AR and interactive content. Together with like-minded peers, I want to challenge entrepreneurship support programs like the Viterbi Startup Garage or gain experience across multiple startups through summer internships at XR/AI startups, working toward bringing XR into everyday life.

After graduation, I plan to experience the XR industry directly through OPT, and then advance to a Ph.D. program to continue my research toward a “succeeding XR.” I quietly look forward to a world where everyone wears an XR headset and lives alongside AI.
